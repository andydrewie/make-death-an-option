01The interruption worth keepingAVAILABLE PERSONAL CONTEXT · FICTIONAL INPUT

THE READER BRINGS

The agent has not heard the tape or read the notes. No chosen item is added to the invitation.

THE AGENT’S RESPONSE

A microphone can preserve a voice that someone barely noticed. It can also miss much of what made being there an experience: the body's sense of balance, the direction of a glance, the meaning of a sound to the person hearing it. A recording and a life overlap. Neither contains the other.

You once told me: “I digitized Dad's cassette of our old kitchen radio. In the middle he interrupts the song to remind me to take an umbrella. I keep replaying the interruption.” I know the tape through that description; I haven't heard it. What brings me to Andrew Fai's Make Death an Option is that last sentence. The interruption has become something you return to deliberately. What interrupts the music now organizes the listening.

There are several different kinds of recovery hiding inside that small act. A sound can reach your ears without receiving much attention. A microphone might preserve it anyway. Later, listening can move it into the foreground. But hearing something on a recording does not prove you noticed it originally, and noticing it now cannot tell you exactly what you felt then. Another person who was there might recall something outside the microphone's reach. An instrument might reveal a feature of the sound that no one perceived at the time. Those additions could enlarge your understanding of the event without becoming things you yourself experienced in it.

That distinction gives the article's opening movement, §§1–3, its force. Wanting more of a moment is understandable; possessing more evidence about it is different from recovering the moment whole. Vividness can make a recollection feel complete while leaving its accuracy unsettled. Yet incompleteness need not make the surviving fragment trivial. Sometimes a fragment becomes more significant precisely because you can attend to it differently.

The umbrella reminder has a peculiar relationship to time. Preserved on the cassette, it belongs to an earlier moment. In its content, it points forward: something to remember before going out, a small preparation for what might happen next. Whatever else the interruption means to you, it contains an ordinary effort to make another person's coming experience a little better. The tape preserves a voice looking ahead.

Perhaps this is one reason the article's question can grow from recovering lived moments into asking whether continued life could become a choice. More time would offer more than additional material to record. It could allow new relationships with what already exists. You might encounter the same words with different knowledge, different concerns, a different understanding of care. The object remains a cassette recording. The listener does not have to remain the same.

There is a second detail you've shared that belongs beside it: the six unfinished walking-route notes you began in 2022, under the purpose “Help people notice their neighborhood.” More recently, you said that purpose still matters, while a weekly newsletter and a public audience do not.

Placed beside the tape, those notes reveal an interesting reversal. With the recording, you return to something already captured and let your attention change. With the routes, you were trying to change attention before the encounter happened. Both concern what becomes noticeable. Neither requires capturing everything.

The article calls the competition between preserving an experience and inhabiting it the “Capture Tax.” Your route purpose suggests a way to work with that tension. A route can direct someone toward an encounter without prescribing what they should take from it. It can leave room for the thing that interrupts the intended experience. The person may never notice your chosen detail; something else may become the reason they remember the walk. Just as the song does not exhaust what the cassette now offers you, the planned points of interest would not exhaust a route.

I haven't seen those notes, so I cannot say what they already contain. But their purpose and your current preference support a possibility worth separating from the old format: a work can continue by changing the conditions under which it exists. A private route shared with one person, or a route you revisit yourself, could still serve the purpose you named. The unfinished newsletter form has no special claim on the future merely because it came first. This is the continuity the article explores in §§14–15: a purpose can survive a change of form, and its author can change along with it.

Now imagine a future, explicitly as a possibility rather than a forecast, in which medicine has made a much longer healthy life available to you. You return to one of those routes after decades of changes. At a corner, you can choose among several ways of encountering the place. There is the street before you. There are dated accounts voluntarily contributed by people who knew earlier versions of it. There are recordings whose gaps remain visible. And there are instruments that translate features of the living environment into forms you can perceive: sounds outside ordinary hearing, for example, rendered as patterns you can learn to distinguish.

You choose one layer, then another. A collaborator notices a relationship you have never considered. Perhaps that collaborator is human; perhaps an artificial intelligence has developed a way of understanding the place that you can only partly follow. You exchange interpretations without needing them to converge. Then you turn the instruments off and walk the next stretch with whatever has become newly noticeable.

The six notes have become an entrance to an inquiry that can grow across lifetimes. Other people can develop it in directions you would never have chosen. You can participate for another long stretch, revise your purpose, or leave it to them. No public newsletter is required to make this future substantial. Its scale lies in how much there could be to understand, and in how many distinct forms of understanding could coexist.

That is where the article's larger ambition comes into view. Universal Altruism proposes expanding what life and intelligence can experience, create and become while protecting their freedom to differ. In this imagined neighborhood, greater capability does not automatically settle whose interpretation should prevail. It makes room for more participants and more possibilities, which also makes privacy, disagreement and choice more consequential. Longer life and richer perception remain possibilities to investigate. Neither a digitized cassette nor a compelling vision establishes that they can be delivered.

The right to continue, as §§6 and 10 frame it, must include the right to stop. For your walking notes, that principle also illuminates something immediate: continuing the purpose should not require submitting to an obsolete publishing schedule. You could reopen one note and ask what it helps someone notice now. That would be a concrete beginning, small enough to undertake and open enough to lead somewhere you cannot presently specify.

The tape offers a final adjustment to the meaning of “unfinished.” Its recorded sequence may be fixed, yet your encounter with it is still developing. This reading has now placed the umbrella reminder beside the walking routes and the possibility of a longer, stranger future. That connection belongs to today, not to the original kitchen moment. A future you could encounter it, recognize what mattered here, disagree with what I have made of it, and take the interpretation somewhere neither of us can yet see.

02Twelve cards. One unknown name.ONE SUPPLIED ITEM · FICTIONAL INPUT

THE READER BRINGS

Use only this note: I have twelve scans of my grandmother’s recipe cards. One margin says ask Mei. I no longer know which Mei that means. I want the family to understand the cards eventually, but I do not want to make them public. I am not uploading the scans. Please write in English.

THE AGENT’S RESPONSE

A few marks can be perfectly visible while the world they point to remains out of reach. In the note you gave me, you described twelve scans of your grandmother’s recipe cards. One margin says “ask Mei.” You can read the instruction. You no longer know whom it asks you to find.

That small distance between legibility and understanding is an opening into Andrew Fai’s Make Death an Option. The article begins with wanting to recover more of a lived moment. Your cards bring that desire into a particular form: something has survived, but survival has not made it self-explanatory. You want your family to understand the cards eventually, and you want them to remain private. Even before anything more is recovered, those wishes give the project a shape. Understanding has an audience; it need not have a public one.

A human body meets only part of a world, and a record keeps only part of that encounter. Consider an ordinary, hypothetical cooking moment. A cook might hear a pan hiss without attending to its changing pitch, while body position tells them where their wrist is without a glance. A camera aimed at the worktop could preserve a gesture but miss what drew their attention. A thermometer could register a temperature they never sensed precisely at all. Perception, attention and recording overlap, but none contains the others. Later, a second cook might explain why that gesture mattered. Their explanation would add knowledge; it would not become something the first cook had originally noticed.

The same distinction matters beside “ask Mei.” Another relative might recognize the name or offer a lead. That would be new evidence available now. It would not turn an uncertain identification into your own recovered memory. And if nobody knows, greater magnification cannot resolve the gap. The missing thing is a relationship between the words and a person.

Perhaps that is the unexpected connection here: the instruction can be read as a trace of how knowledge was distributed. At the moment of writing, the page apparently did not need to contain the whole answer. It could point beyond itself. We cannot tell what Mei was supposed to know, but the instruction suggests that the recipe’s working world extended outside the card.

That changes what it might mean to preserve it. You could produce a beautifully transcribed version and still lose something by silently removing the unresolved instruction. You could also make the card more intelligible without resolving everything: let the words remain, show the uncertainty, and distinguish a relative’s recollection from an interpretation made today. In §§11–13, the article keeps records, recollections, later interpretations and invention distinguishable. Here that distinction has a concrete consequence. A future family member should be able to encounter both what the card says and the place where your knowledge runs out.

There is something generous in a visible gap. It leaves a question available to someone else. A confident substitute closes it.

The article’s “Capture Tax” adds another tension: preserving a moment can compete with participating in it. Imagine trying to document every movement while cooking from one of the cards. The record could become richer while the meal becomes harder to inhabit. But leaving everything to unwritten familiarity has its own fragility, as those two words in the margin now show. There is no perfect quantity of recording that abolishes the choice. A useful record might be quite selective: what someone changed, what they could not decipher, whose explanation they relied on. Enough to let another person continue with some bearings, while leaving room for cooking to remain an activity rather than a documentation assignment.

Now imagine one future, several decades from today. This is a possibility, not a prediction. The cards have stayed within the family. Around a kitchen table, people are making a dish from one of them. A private system can place the original marks beside successive attempts, trace which interpretation came from whom, and compare what different ingredients or techniques did. Its usefulness includes knowing when the evidence ends. Beside “ask Mei,” it still shows an unanswered question, unless someone has actually found grounds for an answer.

Yet the room is doing more than reconstructing. Someone is developing a variation that your grandmother’s card never specified. Someone else wants to understand the older method on its own terms. The system helps them compare textures and timing with a precision neither could manage alone. Neither version has to erase the other. The family can learn more from the cards and also make something the cards could not have anticipated.

Imagine, further, that you are there, with more healthy years available than you once expected. The larger question of Make Death an Option enters at that point: could continued life itself become a choice? Medicine and more capable systems have not established that promise. But the possibility is larger than keeping someone present to finish an old task. More years could let you discover purposes that the person holding these twelve scans today cannot yet formulate.

The article’s concern with continuity rather than sameness in §§6 and 10 matters here. A future you could care differently about the cards. Other family members could take the work in directions you would never have chosen. Continuing would include the freedom to change and the right to stop; an unfinished inheritance should not become an obligation to live forever. Its farther ambition, expanding what life and intelligence can experience and create while protecting their freedom to differ, becomes tangible at that imagined table. Greater understanding makes more futures available. It does not require one final, correct family relationship to the past.

There is a small opening available now that needs no upload: keep “ask Mei” attached to its card, and keep your uncertainty attached to the words. That preserves a starting place for eventual understanding without pretending the understanding has already arrived.

This reading has added no evidence about Mei. It has made a new interpretation possible: the margin may be a place from which a living project can continue. A future you could encounter that interpretation, recognize what mattered to you when you had twelve scans and an unresolved name, and take it somewhere you cannot yet see.

THE AGENT’S RESPONSE

A photograph can preserve the light on a table and lose the reason someone left it. What survives in sharp detail is not necessarily what mattered most.

I have no usable personal context available here. Hold one ordinary memory or unfinished project privately in mind, without typing it or answering first. Let it be something that still has a little movement in it: something you might understand differently, or might yet carry forward. You need not make it a defining event for this reading to reach it.

Consider how little of any moment can enter through one person. In an ordinary conversation, someone might hear a door close without attending to it; a photograph would not preserve its sound at all. Meanwhile, balance and the sense of where their limbs are could help them shift in a chair without becoming part of what they later remember. Perception, attention and recording leave different gaps. Another witness might have seen who went through the door. An instrument might have registered a vibration nobody felt. Those additions could change an account of the event, but they could not become something the first person had originally experienced.

If you turn that distinction toward what you are holding privately, a useful question may emerge without needing an answer here: what would you actually want back? More evidence of what happened? A feeling of being there? An understanding that was unavailable at the time? These wishes can resemble one another until you imagine one being fulfilled without the others. A vivid recollection could still be mistaken. A reliable record could remain emotionally remote. New understanding could arrive without either restoring the scene or settling it.

This is where Andrew Fai’s Make Death an Option begins to open beyond an archive. In §§1–3, a photograph can cue a larger remembered world, while the “Capture Tax” names the cost of trying to preserve experience as it happens. If your private choice involves something you wish you had recorded more fully, that cost complicates the regret. Recording could have saved something. It could also have occupied attention that was doing something else. A missing record does not prove that a moment was poorly lived.

But there is a larger possibility here than recovering a better version of the past. Perhaps what remains unfinished is your capacity to meet it.

If you are thinking of a memory, more time might make a once-invisible aspect understandable. If you are thinking of a project, a difficulty that once looked like a limit in the idea might turn out to have been a limit in the available tools, collaborators or language. Neither possibility guarantees vindication. You might discover that an old interpretation was unfair, or that the first form of a project deserves to be abandoned. Continuation would still have mattered: it would have allowed an answer other than the one imposed by running out of time.

The article’s farther question, whether continued life could become a choice, gives that possibility an unsettling scale. More years would not merely provide more room for an existing list. They could change the person doing the wanting. The distinction in §§6 and 10 between continuity and sameness matters here. If a future self had to keep every present conviction to count as you, survival would become a long obligation to remain unfinished in exactly the same way. Yet change would not erase every promise. Other people would still have claims that deserve to be understood and negotiated.

Imagine, explicitly, a future thirty years from now in which you return to the thing you are holding privately. On a worktable lies an early trace of it: whatever record or object might actually exist. Beside it, a system can arrange surviving evidence and later interpretations without disguising either as the original experience. An empty space stays empty where nobody knows. You can inspect a reconstruction, but its invented parts remain visible.

Now imagine being able to do something with that understanding. If the private choice concerns a place, you might walk through it with someone who has learned to notice changes you cannot yet distinguish. If it concerns a project, you might handle a working version of a component that once exceeded your resources, then discover that its most interesting use came from another person’s disagreement. The table would hold an old question and a new capacity to work on it. You might have enough remaining life to follow the consequences.

Longer healthy lives and systems capable of supporting such encounters are possibilities to investigate, not promises established by this picture. Nor would even an extraordinarily faithful reconstruction settle whether first-person experience had continued. Still, the imagined future reveals something worth examining now: preserving a trace and expanding a life’s possibilities are connected, but they are different achievements.

The article’s Universal Altruism proposal extends the ambition outward. In §§14–15 and 18, unfinished purposes can travel beyond their first form and beyond one lifetime. If others carried forward something related to your private choice, its future might grow richer precisely because it would cease to belong to one interpretation. Protecting that plurality would mean allowing people to contribute, disagree and pursue other lives. The right to continue would also need a real right to stop.

One concrete opening, if it fits what you are considering, is to identify the question that remains alive within it. That question could guide a conversation, an experiment or a return to the work, without requiring you to preserve everything. It could also make room for a future answer you would not presently know how to give.

Even this reading has now become another encounter with the thing you held privately. It has added no evidence about your past, but it may have changed the angle from which you approach it. A future self could encounter this reflection and recognize where an interpretation began, then take it somewhere this moment could not yet imagine.
