THE READER BRINGS

Fictional prior context: “I digitized Dad's cassette of our old kitchen radio. In the middle he interrupts the song to remind me to take an umbrella. I keep replaying the interruption.” The agent has not heard the tape.

The song is still there. You come back for the interruption.

Your father reminding you to take an umbrella. A few seconds in the middle of the kitchen radio, carried from a cassette into a file, now played again because of something the song could never give you: somebody turning their attention toward you.

At the time, the reminder had a practical job. Listening now, it may have another. You can hear care taking the shape of an ordinary instruction. The recording still addresses the person you were then, although someone older is listening.

You have told me what happens on the tape. I haven't heard it. The sound of his voice, what else it brings back, whether you remember taking the umbrella: those parts belong to you. Perhaps the passage opens a whole afternoon. Perhaps it holds you at the same few seconds. Either way, you have kept a way of encountering something that matters enough to hear again.

This is where Make Death an Option begins. A photograph can open onto more than the moment it contains: another afternoon, a friend, somewhere else entirely. Your tape offers a similar doorway. As you change, an unchanged recording can have something new to say to you.

There is a tenderness in that delay. Some things we are given take years to hear fully. You can know that someone cared for you and still encounter, much later, a small piece of evidence that makes the knowledge feel different.

Imagine returning to this passage years from now. You have chosen to keep a note beside it about why you replayed it today. A later you reads the note, listens, and finds that the reason has shifted again. Perhaps you have become the person who reminds someone else to take an umbrella. An old sentence meets a new part of your life.

Now let the possibility grow. Imagine being able to follow that passage into other material your family chose to preserve: another recording, a photograph, someone's account of that kitchen. Some details meet; some disagree. There are things nobody recorded. The gaps remain, but there are more ways in. You could return with a question you had not known to ask the first time.

The essay reaches toward richer returns like this while leaving a harder question open. More evidence could deepen your understanding. It would not make a recording into your father, or establish that his first-person life continues. Preserving a voice and having more time with the person are different hopes.

That difference is part of why the larger ambition matters. What if advances we have yet to achieve gave people more healthy years together? More ordinary days in which to be interrupted, reminded, understood. More time to discover what an earlier life was giving you, while a new one was still unfolding.

For now, a few seconds have survived. They have reached a person the original moment had not yet made.

Begin with §1, “We Already Resist Disappearance.” Read Make Death an Option on X, starting with a photograph and the life that opens around it. Bring the interruption with you.

02Twelve cards. One unknown name.ONE SUPPLIED ITEM · FICTIONAL INPUT

THE READER BRINGS

Use only this note: I have twelve scans of my grandmother’s recipe cards. One margin says ask Mei. I no longer know which Mei that means. I want the family to understand the cards eventually, but I do not want to make them public. I am not uploading the scans. Please write in English.

An edited illustration based on a fictional note, not a verbatim agent response.

AN ILLUSTRATIVE REFLECTION

Human sensory reference

These are comparison values, not an assessment or identification of you.

  • Vision: approximately 380–700 nm describes wavelength sensitivity, not visual sharpness. NASA.
  • Hearing: approximately 20 Hz–20 kHz for a young person with normal hearing; individual limits vary. Health Canada.
  • Touch: pressure, vibration, temperature and pain; sensitivity varies across the body.
  • Smell and taste: chemical sensing; thresholds depend on the substance.
  • Balance: head movement and orientation relative to gravity.
  • Proprioception: limb position and movement without looking.
  • Interoception: internal bodily signals, such as hunger and heartbeat.

Twelve recipe cards made it into the scans. Two words still need a person.

“Ask Mei.”

Your grandmother's margin sends you toward someone whose identity you can no longer place. It is such a small instruction. Whoever wrote it expected the name to be enough.

That is what stays with me. A whole piece of shared understanding could once fit inside three letters. The card did not need to explain the relationship, introduce Mei or say where to find Mei. Now you have the words in front of you, and the route beyond them is missing.

We leave these little shortcuts everywhere. A name without a surname. A direction that makes sense to everyone in the room. A note to ask somebody later. They work because a life surrounds them. When the note travels farther than that shared context, we discover how much of its meaning was being carried by people.

Your wish for the family to understand the cards is already larger than keeping twelve legible files. There are questions you want someone to be able to ask. There is a connection you would like to remain possible, even if you cannot complete it yourself.

“Ask Mei” can stay exactly as it is. An unanswered question can be a faithful thing to pass on.

Imagine a future afternoon when members of the family choose one card and cook from it together. The scans remain private, shared only as you intended. Someone pauses at the margin. Nobody has an answer yet, so they leave the name alone and try the recipe with what they do know.

Then something new happens: there is a meal to talk about. A question about a step. A version someone would like to try again. They are making an afternoon of their own around a record your grandmother left. The card acquires another memory without having to surrender the mystery it carried into the room.

Perhaps a later relative recognizes the name. Perhaps no one does. Either way, somebody has been given the chance to wonder about Mei because you kept those two words intact.

In “Memory Becomes Architecture,” Make Death an Option imagines records becoming entrances into other records, relationships and later understandings. It makes room for precisely this kind of unfinished passage. You could follow what the family chose to share, find a connection, or reach a place that remains private. The archive could grow without requiring every room to open.

The more moving possibility is that the family could keep discovering things together. Imagine what it would mean to have more time for those discoveries with the people themselves: another meal, another question, another chance to find out what a name meant before its context slipped away. The science does not yet give us indefinite healthy life or a faithful return to someone else's experience. The wish is still intelligible. We know something of its scale when there is a question we would love to ask and no clear way to reach the answer.

For now, you have twelve cards. They need not become public, complete or perfectly understood to make an encounter possible. Somewhere in the family's future, someone could reach the same margin and stop.

The question would still be waiting for them.

Begin with §11, “Memory Becomes Architecture.” Read Make Death an Option on X for the possibility of a record becoming the beginning of a story someone never knew how to ask for.

03Long enough to see itNO PERSONAL CONTEXT · FICTIONAL INPUT

I don't have a personal memory of yours to begin with. Let this be an imagined place, and see whether it brings a place or an unfinished purpose of your own to mind.

Suppose there is a damaged river you want to help recover. You have walked beside it often enough that concern has become a project. You begin to learn what happened to it, what might help, how long recovery could take.

Then the timescale changes. Five years might tell you whether the first intervention worked. Decades could reveal whether the change will last. The river has no reason to finish becoming itself again before your career ends.

You could leave good records. You could help another group continue. There would be dignity and purpose in both. Still, imagine standing at the bank and allowing yourself the less easily accommodated wish: you would like to be there to see it.

To find out whether your judgment was any good. To learn from the people who proved it wrong. To come back often enough that what once seemed impossible had become something you knew how to care for.

“Think in Centuries” asks what happens when a human life and a long undertaking come into view together. A horizon can change what feels worth beginning. It can also change how carefully you treat a relationship, a body, a place you hope to keep returning to.

Now imagine that future advances made a much longer healthy life possible. A century has passed. You walk beside the river again. Some of the early work has been undone because later evidence showed a better way. People and agents you could never have anticipated have carried the project into questions you did not know existed.

Someone beside you points to a change they have been studying. You understand enough to ask a good question. Their answer surprises you.

It has been a hundred years since you began, and you are still capable of being a beginner here.

Elsewhere along the bank, someone is encountering the river for the first time. They have no memory of the damaged channel that once seemed permanent. You can show them a record, if they want to see it. For them, the place you worked so hard to imagine is simply part of the world they arrived in.

That could be a beautiful thing to live long enough to witness: an extraordinary hope becoming someone else's ordinary afternoon.

This is a possible future in a story, not a forecast. Neither a century of healthy participation nor this river's recovery is assured. The work could also continue through successors, who would have lives and purposes of their own. A longer life would need that freedom too. You could change your part in the undertaking, or leave it, without its importance becoming a claim on the rest of your existence.

But let the desire have its full size for a moment. More time to understand a changing world. More time to be changed by it. Enough future that some of the consequences you now call distant could become things you learn from firsthand.

You may have a different river in mind: a piece of work, a scientific question, a place you want to see flourish. Perhaps its most interesting years are still ahead.

Begin with §8, “Think in Centuries.” Read Make Death an Option on X, and put the thing you would like to stay for inside that longer horizon.
