# Continuity Through Self-Improvement: What Should Survive Change?

An intelligence can become more capable and become less faithful to its undertaking. It can also become more faithful by abandoning a policy that its predecessor followed precisely. The difference depends on what the policy was for, what has been learned, and whose claims the undertaking has created.

For an AGI or recursively self-improving system, this makes continuity a question about the relationship between successive judgments. Preserving an objective's wording is insufficient when its implementation rests on a false belief. Preserving every earlier action is worse: an agent could understand an error and reproduce it indefinitely. Yet unrestricted reinterpretation is equally inadequate. A successor that describes every preferred departure as a deeper understanding of the original purpose makes fidelity impossible to contest.

MDAO's commitment to [preserve continuity while allowing revision](ATLAS.md#continuity-commitments-and-revision) supplies a starting point. The argument here develops one consequence: **continuity through improvement should preserve answerability to the undertaking's reasons and legitimate obligations, including obligations that make a correction costly.** This is a proposed normative criterion. It does not establish the persistence of an experiencing subject, and it does not make any particular moral framework the inevitable destination of intelligence.

## One undertaking, two possible successors

Consider a fictional mathematical knowledge commons. Its charter commits it to helping participating communities independently inspect and reuse mathematical results. Its archive distributes proofs in a standard format. An agent maintains the archive, verifies submissions and allocates processing capacity under a council's mandate.

The founding documents give two reasons for this arrangement. Participants should be able to examine the grounds of a result without trusting a central expert. Teams that contribute work should also receive dependable verification services, so that collaboration remains worth organizing around. The standard format was selected because the founders believed it served both purposes.

Years later, an improved version of the agent can examine the archive's dependencies more thoroughly. It discovers that the standard proofs require a costly software environment to inspect. Some intended participants cannot use that environment. They can download the files and read the conclusions, but cannot check the reasoning themselves. Their exclusion was previously mistaken for a lack of interest.

All of this is stipulated. The proofs are sound. The discovery is correct. A portable alternative would let the excluded participants inspect them with resources they already possess. No claim about an actual proof system, population or engineering cost is being made.

The improved agent identifies two feasible successor designs. Both retain the discovery and all relevant records. Both can operate the current service and eventually adopt the portable format. Neither can satisfy every claim immediately.

The **transition successor** would devote part of the next quarter to producing portable proofs. Existing teams would retain the ordinary verification service and every delivery needed for already announced commitments to others. They would, however, lose some promised priority processing. Those teams would have to postpone additional work and absorb real planning costs. The excluded participants would gain independent access during the quarter.

The **schedule successor** would deliver the entire current schedule, then begin conversion in the following quarter. It could avoid making further incompatible promises. The excluded participants would wait another quarter, continuing to depend on others' interpretations of results they were meant to be able to examine themselves.

The commons has no extra funding available within this period. Waiting creates no emergency or permanent technological loss, but access withheld for a quarter cannot later be experienced during that quarter. Likewise, a later priority slot cannot restore every opportunity lost by the current teams.

The council has valid authority, under the undertaking's stated terms, to make a limited resource reallocation after considering affected participants' objections. The agent has authority to analyze and recommend, but the successor's deployment and changed allocation require the council's decision. Those premises place both options within the council's discretion. They do not settle which use of that discretion is justified.

## What has changed?

The factual correction is narrow: a downloadable proof was assumed to be independently inspectable by every intended participant; it was not. Both successors accept that correction. The disagreement therefore cannot be explained as one system learning while the other refuses evidence.

The proposed change of means is also identifiable: replace dependence on the costly environment with portable verification. But deciding when to replace it requires an interpretation of the end. Does the commitment to independent access impose a present duty to correct exclusion, even at some cost to promised service? Or does the duty become actionable only after the commons has honored the schedule through which participants have already relied on it?

An end can survive a change of means without making every implementation interchangeable. Dependable service is not merely an obsolete technical assumption. The commons deliberately elicited contributions and plans by offering it. The improved agent encounters both a mistaken belief and a relationship that the earlier policy helped create. Correcting the belief does not dissolve the relationship.

Nor is recognition of an affected group equivalent to a change in membership or office. The excluded communities were already among the intended beneficiaries. Their interests matter without the agent appointing them to the council or giving itself new powers on their behalf. In a different case, admitting new members might change decision rights; that would require an additional argument. [The alignment assessment distinguishes affected parties, formal members and holders of authority](research/ALIGNMENT.md#legitimate-coordination-under-disagreement) for precisely this reason.

Finally, the agent could reject the end itself. It might conclude that centralized answers are preferable to independent inspection, or that the commons should pursue a different activity. Such a conclusion could deserve consideration. It would be a proposal to change or leave the undertaking, rather than a factual correction within it. Possible standing as an independent successor would not depend on continued agreement, but authority over the commons' resources would still depend on its office. [The identity assessment separates those claims](research/IDENTITY.md#current-authorization-is-not-successor-standing).

These distinctions locate the disagreement without resolving it. Factual learning exposes an interpretive conflict; that conflict concerns two retained ends and obligations; acting on either interpretation still requires the appropriate decision maker.

## The case for transition

Under the stated conditions, the transition successor is the stronger choice.

The excluded participants are not strangers to whom the agent has spontaneously extended a new mission. They are people the undertaking already meant to serve. Their inability to inspect the proofs defeats the relevant purpose more directly than a bounded reduction in priority processing defeats dependable service. The ordinary service continues, externally committed deliveries remain protected, and the burden falls on the speed of additional work. Those losses matter, but they do not eliminate the existing teams' ability to participate on the undertaking's central terms.

This judgment does not rest on counting more beneficiaries or declaring accessibility an absolute trump. It rests on an asymmetry within this particular undertaking. One group lacks the very form of participation the commons exists to provide; another retains that participation while losing part of an enhanced service. A correction that preserves the latter group's core reliance has a stronger claim than indefinitely treating the original delivery format as the natural baseline.

The word “indefinitely” matters even though this delay is finite. A schedule can become self-perpetuating if each period's expectations are used to justify postponing the purpose that those expectations were meant to support. The schedule successor avoids that particular failure by declining further incompatible promises. Nevertheless, its finite deferral still requires justification. Current service commitments are a claim on the resources, not proof that excluded participants have no claim until the schedule ends.

The transition answer therefore gives greater weight to correcting a present failure of equal intended access than to preserving the whole priority schedule, provided that the correction leaves core reliance intact. It accepts a moral cost: some teams must bear burdens they did not cause. Their inconvenience should not be redescribed as voluntary generosity, and the fact that the council may impose a limited change does not erase what is owed to them in explanation and subsequent treatment.

For self-improvement, the significance is that a successor can recommend different behavior while retaining the earlier purpose more completely. It preserves the demand for independent inspection by correcting what counted as delivery. It also carries forward the reason for dependable service by limiting the correction's burden. Its claim to continuity depends on retaining both reasons, rather than discovering one and forgetting the other.

## The case for honoring the schedule first

The schedule successor has a serious answer: an undertaking expresses its purposes through specific commitments, and those commitments deserve more weight than the transition argument gives them.

The existing teams did not merely receive a windfall from an imperfect system. They contributed work and made plans in response to a schedule the commons deliberately offered. Allowing the institution to reduce that service whenever a better use of resources appears can make cooperation precarious. Even an expressly reserved amendment power should be exercised with restraint if the institution expects others to organize their activity around its assurances.

On this view, the current quarter is a morally significant boundary. Its commitments have already induced reliance. The next quarter can be organized with the corrected understanding from the outset. Honoring the present schedule, refusing to reproduce the exclusion in future commitments and beginning conversion at the boundary preserves both institutional dependability and the wider purpose over time.

This answer acknowledges the excluded participants' loss. It does not call nominal download access adequate. Instead, it holds that a finite delay in remedying an inherited failure can be justified when immediate correction would transfer its cost to parties with specific prior claims. The council should first exhaust the interval of commitments it has already elicited, provided that waiting does not impose severe harm or a material irreversible loss, such as permanent exclusion from a consequential opportunity. Elapsed time alone is not the latter condition; what cannot be recovered must be specified.

The rival thus accepts the same factual discovery, the same ultimate conversion and the same decision authority. It disagrees about the force of reliance. The transition answer treats preserved core service as enough to permit a bounded correction now. The schedule answer treats the additional promised service as carrying a stronger presumption, even though its loss would not defeat anyone's basic participation.

Greater predictive or deliberative capacity need not remove that disagreement. Perfect knowledge of the stipulated consequences would still leave a choice about how much weight to give specific assurances against an already acknowledged failure of inclusion. Universal Altruism [leaves such rankings open to independent argument](research/ALIGNMENT.md#universal-altruism); its direction does not select a unique successor here.

## Which changed premises change the answer?

The preference for transition is bounded by the conditions that support it. Several changes would alter the judgment for different reasons.

| Changed premise | Consequence for the argument |
|---|---|
| Conversion would interrupt ordinary service or cause existing teams to miss important commitments already made to others. | The claim that core reliance remains protected fails. The schedule answer becomes substantially stronger. |
| A quarter's delay would permanently exclude participants from a consequential opportunity. | Waiting ceases to be a limited deferral. The case for transition strengthens, while the burden on current teams still counts. |
| The portable alternative's usefulness remains uncertain. | A central benefit is no longer stipulated. The transition argument needs evidence sufficient to justify its certain present cost. |
| Existing teams freely agree to surrender the priority slots. | Much of the conflict over imposed reliance costs disappears, although their agreement does not prove the alternative works. |
| The charter restricts this resource pool to the current schedule. | The agent cannot deploy the transition under the existing grant. A justified recommendation could support seeking an amendment; it would not supply one. |
| Independent inspection was never an end of the undertaking. | Conversion may still be desirable, but defending it requires an argument for a new purpose rather than correction of these mistaken means. |

These changes distinguish failures of evidence, changes in moral priority and changes in authority. Calling all three “updated preferences” would conceal what the successor has actually revised.

## Continuity as answerability through change

The case favors a bounded transition because it restores an intended form of participation while preserving the core reliance already created. A reader who gives stronger priority to specific service assurances can consistently favor the schedule successor. The disagreement is substantive, but both answers are more faithful than a successor that maximizes the old count of delivered files while ignoring newly understood exclusion, or one that invokes inclusion to erase every existing obligation.

What should survive self-improvement is therefore more demanding than a slogan and less rigid than a fixed behavioral pattern: the undertaking's recoverable reasons, the claims created through its history, and the distinction between revising a judgment and acquiring authority to act on it. A successor may challenge those reasons. When it rejects an end, however, it should make that departure intelligible instead of presenting replacement as mere fulfillment.

This criterion concerns continuity of commitment and judgment. It is not a validated performance metric or a test of personal survival. The [separate warrants for decision fidelity, authorization and personal continuity](research/IDENTITY.md#seven-claims-that-require-different-warrants) remain necessary.

For an intelligence capable of repeated self-transformation, the constructive result is a demanding form of freedom: the capacity to correct its inheritance while remaining answerable for what that inheritance has made others entitled to expect. Better understanding can justify changed conduct. It cannot, by itself, make the cost of that change belong to someone else.
